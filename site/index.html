Nalanda.

From the fifth century CE

A city of brick where monks and scholars lived and studied in their thousands. Alongside philosophy they learned grammar, medicine, logic, mathematics and astronomy, and its library drew pilgrims from as far as China.

Traditional accounts speak of 10,000 students and 2,000 teachers. Nalanda was a Buddhist monastery and place of learning; the sky was one of many things studied here.

Before 500 BCE

First, they kept time.

The oldest text we have, the Vedanga Jyotisha attributed to Lagadha, is a calendar. It follows the sun and the moon through 27 groups of stars, the nakshatras, so that each ritual fell on the right day.

The surviving text is dated to the last centuries BCE and may carry a tradition from around 700 to 600 BCE.

The first centuries CE

Then the sky was divided into twelve.

The twelve signs and the birth chart enter Indian texts. By the sixth century Varahamihira of Ujjain had set the system down in the Brihat Jataka.

Scholars still debate how much arrived through exchange with Greek astronomers and how much grew here.

बृहत् पराशर होरा शास्त्र

Parashara.

Tradition says the sage Parashara taught this knowledge to his student Maitreya. The Brihat Parashara Hora Shastra, the fullest classical text on reading a birth chart, carries his name.

Scholars date the text as we have it to some time after 600 CE. The drawing is an artist's impression; no portrait of Parashara exists.
